Abstract
Photoproducts of the photosensitizer protoporphyrin IX (PpIX) may have diag-
nostic and therapeutic relevance in photodynamic diagnosis (PDD) and photody-
namic therapy (PDT), as they possess fluorescence and photosensitizing properties 
and could serve as a complementary dosimetry index alongside photosensitizer 
photobleaching. Consequently, a detailed characterization of these photoproducts 
is necessary for exploring their potential application in PDD and PDT. Further, 
understanding the processes of photobleaching and photoproduct formation will 
be useful for diagnosis and treatment planning in PDD and PDT. In this study, 
the formation of four photoproducts previously observed from the irradiation of 
PpIX-­dimethyl ester was extended to investigate their formation from PpIX. PpIX 
in solution was irradiated at 405 and 635 nm, wavelengths relevant to PDD and 
PDT, and the formation of the photoproducts was analyzed using liquid chroma-
tography–tandem mass spectrometry (LC–MS/MS). Hydroxyaldehyde and formyl 
photoproducts, and their isomeric forms, were identified, and their fold increases 
were evaluated under different irradiation conditions. Although the physico-
chemical properties of photoproducts depend on the environment, this solution-­
based study provides fundamental understanding of the photochemical reaction 
of PpIX and its photoproducts under clinically relevant irradiation conditions and 
may aid studies aimed at exploiting photoproducts for improved dosimetry and 
therapeutic outcomes in PDD and PDT.

INTRODUCTION
Appropriate dose estimation is important for improved 
diagnosis and treatment in photodynamic diagnosis 
(PDD) and photodynamic therapy (PDT). In PDD and 
PDT that use protoporphyrin IX (PpIX) as a photosensi-
tizer, PpIX photobleaching serves as a useful index for an 
implicit method of dosimetry.1–3 PpIX photobleaching is 
accompanied by the formation of its photoproducts. It 
has been proposed that monitoring of these photoprod-
ucts could serve as a complementary dosimetry index 
to photobleaching.4,5 In addition, these photoproducts 
may have diagnostic and therapeutic relevance due to 
their fluorescence properties and their photosensitizing 
effects, resulting in prolonged diagnostic and photo-
sensitizing effects in PDD and PDT.6–12 Therefore, their 
characterization and analysis are important for under-
standing their potential biological implications in PDD 
and PDT. A deeper understanding of these photoprod-
ucts may support the enhancement of treatment proto-
cols and improve dosimetry estimation and prediction 
of phototoxic effects through better monitoring of pho-
tosensitizer dynamics. However, a detailed structural 
analysis of these photoproducts and their relative for-
mation under PDD and PDT remains limited and dated. 
Inhoffen et al.13 investigated the formation of hydroxy-
aldehyde photoproduct by irradiating PpIX dimethyl 
ester (PpIX-­DME) in methylene chloride and character-
ized two isomers of the photoproduct. Cox and Whitten 
also studied the formation of these photoproducts in 
solution14 and in organized media,8 and also reported 
the generation of formyl photoproducts from irradiated 
PpIX-­DME.8,14
In our previous studies, spectroscopic and mass spec-
trometric analyses of the photobleaching and photooxida-
tion of PpIX in an organic solvent upon irradiation with 
405-­ and 635-­nm lights were performed.15,16 The forma-
tion of hydroxyaldehyde photoproducts and formyl pho-
toproducts of PpIX was observed, and the dependence of 
the photoproducts formation on wavelength and fluence 
rate was noted.
The present study extends the analysis of the pre-
viously identified photoproducts of PpIX-­DME to ex-
amine their formation from PpIX under conditions 
relevant to PDT and PDD. The isomeric forms of these 
photoproducts were separated and identified using liq-
uid chromatography–mass spectrometry (LC–MS), and 
a detailed structural analysis of these photoproducts 
formed upon PpIX irradiation is presented. The physi-
cochemical properties of PpIX photoproducts may vary 
depending on the environment, as factors such as light 
dose, oxygen availability, pH, photosensitizer concen-
tration, and intracellular localization, and the presence 
of oxidizable biomolecules can affect their formation 
mechanism.10,17 Given the multiple interacting factors, 
analysis in a controlled environment is necessary to 
elucidate the photochemical processes. However, ma-
nipulating these factors in vivo is difficult,2 making it 
necessary to first characterize photoproduct formation 
in a simple system, such as a solution, before analysis 
in a complex biological environment. Thus, the result of 
this study is useful for understanding the reaction pro-
cesses of photoproducts in biological environments and 
supports future studies aimed at exploiting photoprod-
ucts for improved dosimetry and therapeutic outcomes 
in PDD and PDT.
MATERIALS AND METHODS
Sample irradiation
Protoporphyrin IX (P8293, Sigma-­Aldrich, USA), diluted to 
10 μM in dimethyl sulfoxide (D4540, Sigma-­Aldrich), was 
irradiated at a wavelength of 635 nm or 405 nm using the ir-
radiation setup described in previous work.15 Our previous 
study showed a higher fluorescence emission from photo-
products for PpIX irradiated at a lower fluence rate for the 
irradiation wavelength of 635 nm, and vice versa for the ir-
radiation wavelength of 405 nm.16 Thus, PpIX irradiation 
was performed with a wavelength of 635 nm at 10 mW/
cm2 for 100, 120, and 140 J/cm2 and with a wavelength of 
405 nm at 100 mW/cm2 for 10 and 14 J/cm2 to generate the 
photoproducts. Photoproduct formation after PpIX irradia-
tion at these wavelengths has been previously confirmed 
using fluorescence spectroscopy.15 Sample preparation and 
analyses were performed in the dark to avoid uncontrolled 
photochemical changes in the samples.
High-­performance liquid chromatography 
(HPLC) analysis
PpIX before and after irradiation were chromatographi-
cally separated using HPLC (Vanquish Flex, Thermo 
Scientific), with an LC column (Hypersil GOLD C18, 
1.9 μm, 2.1 × 150 mm, Thermo Scientific) at 40°C. The in-
jection volume was 2 μL, and the flow rate was maintained 
at 0.3 mL/min. The mobile phase consisted of A: distilled 
water with 0.1% formic acid and B: acetonitrile with 0.1% 
formic acid. The gradient was started at A:B (v/v) ratio 
50:50, linearly changed to 2:98 by 14 min, and maintained 
at 2:98 until 15 min. The ratio was then returned to 50:50 
at 15 min and maintained at 50:50 until 23 min. The gra-
dient beyond 14 min was used for column washing and 
re-­equilibration.

Mass spectrometric analysis
The output of the HPLC was analyzed using an Orbitrap 
mass spectrometer (Orbitrap Exploris 240, Thermo 
Scientific). Full MS method with positive and negative 
mode switching, targeted MS/MS, and data-­dependent 
MS/MS were performed with a scan range between 
m/z 200 and m/z 1000. The MS/MS analyses were per-
formed at normalized collision energies (NCEs) of 40% 
and 60%.
It was assumed that similar formation mechanisms 
as those of hydroxyaldehyde and formyl photoproducts 
formed from PpIX-­DME irradiation would occur during 
PpIX irradiation. Thus, analyses were performed at the 
m/z of the protonated monoisotopic peak of PpIX (A), as 
well as those of the hydroxyaldehyde and formyl photo-
products (B–E) previously reported for PpIX-­DME,13,14 il-
lustrated in Figure 1. Table 1 summarizes the oxygenation 
steps of PpIX that resulted in the formation of photoprod-
ucts B–E.
RESULTS AND DISCUSSION
Figure 2 shows the total ion chromatograms (TICs) and 
the extracted ion chromatograms (EICs) of PpIX before 
and after irradiation. The EICs were obtained at the m/z of 
the protonated monoisotopic ion of PpIX (m/z 563.3) and 
that of photoproduct B (m/z 595.25), C (m/z 565.24), D 
(m/z 597.23), and E (m/z 567.22) with a mass tolerance of 
±5 ppm. The extracted ion chromatogram of PpIX before 
irradiation (Figure 2A) shows minor amounts of the four 
photoproducts (B–E) present in PpIX before irradiation. 
The photoproducts may have formed due to unintended 
exposure of PpIX to stray light during the manufacturing 
or handling process before the analyses. A notable de-
crease in the NL intensity of PpIX is observed following 
irradiation with both 635 nm (Figure 2B–D) and 405 nm 
(Figure 2E,F) light, indicating the photobleaching of the 
photosensitizer. An increase in the NL of some photo-
products was seen after irradiation (Figure 2B–F), indi-
cating their formation is due to the photomodification of 
PpIX.
Laser irradiation with a wavelength of 
635 nm
The formation of the hydroxyaldehyde photoproduct, 
photoprotoporphyrin (Ppp), was seen following PpIX ir-
radiation with 635 nm laser light (Figure  2B–D). The 
generation of Ppp from PpIX irradiation with a 635 nm 
light has been previously confirmed through fluores-
cence and absorption analyses.9,16,18 The EIC of photo-
product B indicates two major isomers of Ppp (B1 and 
B2) reported by Inhoffen et al.13 and the formation of two 
possible additional isomers (B3 and B4) in minor quan-
tities. An increase in the EIC signal intensity of photo-
products C, D, and E was also observed after irradiation 
with 635 nm. The EIC indicates the presence of two iso-
mers each for photoproduct C (2-­formyl-­4-­vinyldeutero
porphyrin IX and 4-­formyl-­2-­vinyldeuteroporphyrin IX) 
and D (photo-­4-­formyl-­2-­vinyldeuteroporphyrin IX and 
photo-­2-­formyl-­4-­vinyldeuteroporphyrin IX). The peak 
areas of each photoproduct under each PpIX irradiation 
condition are shown in Figure 3. Consistent with the TIC 
result (Figure  2), more photoproducts were formed fol-
lowing PpIX irradiation at 635 nm. When comparing MS 
area peak intensities after irradiation fluence of 120 J/cm2 
(Figure 3), isomers B1 and B2 of photoproduct B exhibited 
the highest signals among all photoproducts. Isomers B3 
and B4, as well as the two isomers of photoproduct C (C1 
and C2), were detected in moderate intensities, while the 
two isomers of photoproduct D (D1 and D2) and photo-
product E were detected with relatively low intensities. 
However, since the ionization and detection efficiency can 
vary depending on the molecular species, it is difficult to 
determine the relative abundance of these photoproducts. 
The amount of photoproducts formed after irradiation 
was compared by the increase in the peak intensity before 
and after irradiation. The two isomers B3 and B4 showed 
the highest fold increase (≈38-­fold and 47-­fold, respec-
tively), followed by the isomers D1 and D2 (≈28-­fold). In 
contrast, isomers B1 and B2 increased by ≈10-­fold, while 
C1, C2, and E showed a lesser increase, ranging between 
≈2.5 and 3.7-­fold. Although B1 and B2 had the highest ab-
solute intensities, B3 and B4 demonstrated a more notable 
relative increase.

Laser irradiation with a wavelength of 
405 nm
Comparing the EICs of PpIX before (Figure 2A) and after 
irradiation with a wavelength of 405 nm (Figure  2E,F), a 
reduction, rather than an increase, in the EIC intensities of 
the photoproducts was observed. After the irradiation with a 
fluence of 10 J/cm2, the EIC intensity of photoproduct B de-
creased to 10.25% (Figure 2E). This reduction may indicate 
that either the pre-­existing Ppp was photobleached with ir-
radiation, while no new Ppp was generated, or that any Ppp 
that was formed was rapidly converted to another compound. 
The study by Dysart and Patterson2 reports the formation 
of other photoproducts from Ppp. Further, Ppp, generated 
from irradiated PpIX-­DME, showed the highest extinction 
coefficient at 422 and 436 nm for the photoproduct isomers 
B1 and B2, respectively.13 However, the absorption bands of 
these photoproducts likely extend into the irradiation region 
of 405 nm due to the broad nature of the absorption peaks. 
Additionally, the 405 nm laser used for irradiation also has 
a full width at half maximum of 7 nm, which may overlap 
with the absorption tail of Ppp. Consequently, Ppp may have 
undergone rapid photoreactions during 405 nm irradiation.
After similar irradiation with a fluence of 10 J/cm2, the 
EIC intensities of photoproducts C, D, and E were reduced 
to 83.00%, 71.85%, and 90.11%, respectively (Figure 2E). 
These reductions in the formyl photoproducts (photo-
products C–E) were less notable compared with that of 
Ppp. Figure 3 also shows no notable formation of photo-
products D1, D2, and E, with the photobleaching of the 
photoproducts after PpIX irradiation with 405 nm. The for-
mation rate of the formyl photoproduct is approximately 
10 times lower than that of Ppp.19 Since the photoproducts 
also undergo photobleaching with irradiation as they are 
formed,10,14,20,21 a reduction in their EIC intensities is ex-
pected if their photobleaching rate exceeds their forma-
tion rate. Photoproduct C is generated via the oxygenation 
of PpIX, and its irradiation is expected to lead to the for-
mation of the hydroxyaldehyde photoproduct D. Analysis 
using PpIX-­DME showed that the irradiation of photo-
product C results in the formation of photoproduct D with 
an absorption band at 685 nm.22 Further, photoproduct E 
is expected to be generated, although it is formed in quan-
tities less than that of photoproduct D.22 The photooxida-
tion of photoproduct C is much slower than PpIX,22 which 
may account for the low levels of photoproducts D and E.

Neutral loss scanning of MS/MS spectra before and after 
PpIX irradiation showed increased protonated monoisotopic 
peaks of C35H36N4O4, C34H34N4O5, and C35H38N4O4 in sam-
ples irradiated with a wavelength of 405 nm. These peaks 
had higher intensities at lower irradiation fluence (10 J/cm2) 
compared with higher irradiation fluence (14 J/cm2). Thus, 
it may be presumed that these observed compounds may be 
intermediate photoproducts or photofragments of PpIX pho-
toproducts. The peaks of these compounds were not notable 
under 635 nm irradiation of PpIX. The chemical structures of 
these compounds remain to be elucidated.
MS/MS
The fragmentation patterns of PpIX and photoproducts 
B–E were analyzed by MS/MS (Figures 4–8), and the ob-
tained spectra were compared.
As observed in the LC–MS/MS spectra of PpIX 
(Figure 4), PpIX fragmentation occurs through the loss of 
its two propionic acid side chains, as illustrated in the an-
notated PpIX chemical structure. Similar fragmentation 
patterns of PpIX have been previously reported.23 
Photoproduct B undergoes loss of the oxygenated vinyl 
ring and subsequently follows a fragmentation pathway 
similar to that of PpIX by fragmenting the propionic acid 
side chains. The similarity in fragmentation patterns be-
tween PpIX and photoproduct B suggests that they share a 
largely similar chemical structure, with the key difference 
being the oxidative modification of the vinyl group in pho-
toproduct B.
Two distinct MS/MS fragmentation patterns were ob-
served among the four isomeric photoproducts of B. B1 
and B2, and B3 and B4 exhibited identical spectra, in-
dicating possible positional isomerism. Fragmentation 
of B1 and B2, and B3 and B4, is characterized by the 
initial loss of CHO and CH2O, respectively, resulting 
in a consistent 1 Da mass difference in the fragmented 
compound between the two pairs. This recurring mass 
shift indicates possible hydrogen rearrangements or 
differences in conjugation within the macrocyclic core. 
These spectral differences, observed under both NCE 
conditions, support the presence of two distinct struc-
tural cores, each yielding a pair of positional isomers.23

Irradiation of PpIX causes the formation of hydroxyal-
dehyde and formyl photoproducts of PpIX (photoproducts 
B–E). These photoproducts may be generated in varying 
quantities depending on the irradiation conditions. In our 
previous study, we observed that hydroxyaldehyde photo-
products appeared dominant under red-­light irradiation, 
whereas formyl photoproducts were more prominent under 
blue-­light irradiation.16 This conclusion was based on ab-
sorption and fluorescence spectroscopy. However, LC–MS/
MS analysis in the present study indicates that both types 
of photoproducts are prominent under red-­light irradia-
tion. PpIX and photoproducts C and E have fairly similar 
absorption spectra, which makes distinguishing one from 
the other difficult using spectroscopic methods alone.22
In the present study, the primary aim was the iden-
tification and characterization of PpIX photoproducts 
rather than to reproduce a specific clinical protocol.

All identified PpIX photoproducts are effective sen-
sitizers of singlet oxygen,7,22,29 emit fluorescence,10,12 
and may contribute to enhancing PDT and PDD effi-
cacy. Similar photoproducts have been observed in ho-
mogeneous solutions,17 organized media,17 and both 
in vitro1,2,10 and in vivo environments,30–32 though their 
quantities vary depending on conditions.21,22 The mi-
croenvironment, such as oxygen levels, pH, tempera-
ture, and compartmentalization in cells and tissues, as 
well as external factors such as the wavelength and the 
fluence rate, can influence the regioselectivity of sin-
glet oxygen addition to PpIX, favoring the formation of 
either hydroxyaldehyde or formyl photoproducts.19,22 
For example, heterogeneous oxygen distribution may 
influence the relative contribution of type I and type II 
photochemical reactions,33 potentially affecting photo-
product yield. Further, PpIX and its photoproducts may 
bind to biomolecules and may stabilize or quench inter-
mediates, or lead to secondary reactions with biomole-
cules, thereby altering photoproduct stability compared 
with analysis in solution conditions.34 These factors 
should be considered when translating solution-­based 
findings to biological systems, and in future analyses 
aimed at the clinical utilization of photoproducts in 
PDD and PDT.
CONCLUSION
In this study, PpIX was analyzed in solution to investigate 
whether its photoproducts resemble those previously 
reported for PpIX-­DME13,14 and to evaluate the relative 
quantities formed under conditions relevant to PDD and 
PDT. The results of the present study confirm the pres-
ence of hydroxyaldehyde and formyl photoproducts and 
reveal their formation in isomeric forms under PDD and 
PDT irradiation conditions. These results provide a basis 
for future studies on photoproduct formation in more 
complex biological environments, where microenviron-
mental factors may influence photoproduct distribution 
and photodynamic efficacy. It can also provide insight 
into understanding the reaction of PpIX under clinically 
relevant irradiation conditions and may aid studies aimed 
at exploiting photoproducts for improved dosimetry and 
therapeutic outcomes in PDD and PDT.
